## Design Specificity Verdict

**LLM assessment:** Specific, not generic. The heated-workshop visual language, misconception vocabulary, ember palette, and live economics question give the page a recognisable point of view. The main generic pressure comes from familiar SaaS patterns around multi-audience navigation, “sign up” conversion, and repeated product-preview sections.

**Deterministic scan:** 3 advisory findings in `index.html`: Inter is flagged as an overused font; 123 em-dashes are flagged as copy-cadence saturation; 5 aphoristic constructions are flagged. The font finding is likely intentional because the documented design system explicitly uses Inter for body/UI. The copy findings are worth reviewing, especially where several sections use “No hints…” / “not X, but Y” contrast patterns.

**Visual overlay:** Not injected. The available browser workflow permits read-only inspection, so this run used fresh desktop/mobile screenshots and DOM snapshots instead of mutating the page with an overlay.

## Overall Impression

Forge has a compelling product idea and a notably clear visual metaphor. The hero earns attention because it makes a concrete promise, then immediately lets visitors try a real exam-style question. Desktop feels composed and intentional. On mobile, however, the first viewport becomes a long persuasion stack: navigation, eyebrow, headline, lede, CTAs, supporting line, and consent card. The strongest proof arrives later than it should, and the homepage then broadens into a very large multi-audience narrative.

## What’s Working

- The headline and supporting copy explain the learning outcome, not merely the feature: wrong answers become targeted repair.
- The interactive Economics demo is excellent above-the-fold proof. It makes the “misconception” claim tangible and uses a real exam concept rather than a generic placeholder.
- The visual system is distinctive and coherent. Warm ember orange against dark workshop surfaces gives the brand a memorable atmosphere without sacrificing legibility.

## Priority Issues

### P1 — Mobile proof arrives too late

**Why it matters:** At 390px wide, the first viewport is almost entirely copy and consent UI; the interactive question card starts below it. A student deciding whether this is useful has to keep scrolling before seeing the product’s best evidence.

**Fix:** Shorten the mobile lede to two or three lines, move the demo card immediately after the primary CTA, or use a compact “Try one question” preview that can expand into the full card. Keep the supporting exam-board line below the proof.

**Suggested command:** `/impeccable adapt the landing hero for mobile so the interactive question proof appears in the first viewport`

### P1 — The landing page serves too many audiences at once

**Why it matters:** The nav exposes student, teacher, school, class, login, and signup paths, while the body presents student and teacher product previews plus subject discovery and early access. This is useful coverage, but it makes the primary visitor journey less decisive.

**Fix:** Choose one primary entry audience for this page, then make the other audience a quieter secondary route or a dedicated landing page. For a student-led homepage, keep “Try the quiz” dominant and move teacher/school routes into a single secondary menu or section.

**Suggested command:** `/impeccable clarify the landing page’s primary audience and reduce competing navigation paths`

### P2 — Consent UI has become a hero-level block

**Why it matters:** The banner is correctly non-obstructive and aligned under the supporting line, but it occupies substantial hero height, especially on mobile. Its prominence is close to the primary product proof even though analytics consent is secondary to the page goal.

**Fix:** Keep it inline, but reduce the explanatory copy and use a more compact desktop row. On mobile, preserve the two actions while collapsing the explanation behind a “Why?” disclosure or shorter sentence.

**Suggested command:** `/impeccable polish the inline analytics consent card so it remains clear but takes less hero space`

### P2 — Secondary CTA has too much visual parity

**Why it matters:** “Join the waiting list” is a valid fallback, but its pill treatment is nearly as prominent as “Try the quiz — free.” That splits attention before visitors have experienced the product.

**Fix:** Make the quiz CTA the unmistakable primary action. Render the waiting-list action as a text link or low-contrast tertiary button, and repeat it later near the email form.

**Suggested command:** `/impeccable sharpen the landing hero CTA hierarchy around trying the quiz first`

### P2 — Copy cadence feels over-engineered in places

**Why it matters:** The detector’s 123 em-dashes and 5 aphoristic constructions are advisory, not proof of a visual defect, but the repeated contrast rhythm can make the voice feel manufactured across a long page.

**Fix:** Keep the strongest Forge-specific lines, then vary sentence structure in the loop and mode sections. Replace some em-dashes with direct sentences or concrete examples, especially in dense explanatory blocks.

**Suggested command:** `/impeccable refine the landing page copy for a more natural rhythm while preserving the Forge voice`

## Persona Red Flags

- **Jordan, first-time student:** The product is appealing, but the first mobile screen asks for sustained reading before showing the question. The waiting-list CTA may also distract from the immediate free trial.
- **Sam, teacher evaluating adoption:** The teacher value proposition is present, but it is mixed into the student journey. Sam may need a clearer, faster route to classroom proof and setup details.
- **Alex, school decision-maker:** School navigation is visible in the header, but the homepage’s strongest proof is student-level. Alex may not immediately see implementation, safeguarding, or rollout evidence without exploring.

## Minor Observations

- The “GCSE & A Level · Spec-mapped · Built by a practising teacher” eyebrow is useful credibility, but it wraps awkwardly on mobile; consider shortening or splitting it into a smaller two-part treatment.
- The section numbering and repeated eyebrow labels help the Forge system feel authored, but there are enough of them that some could be removed without losing orientation.
- The demo footer is appropriately transparent about illustrative/live-bank context, which protects trust.
- The mobile header is compact and functional; the menu label is clear, but the desktop audience dropdowns add considerable cognitive load before the hero begins.

## Questions to Consider

- Should this homepage optimize first for students trying a question, or for teachers evaluating classroom use?
- Is the waiting list still a co-primary conversion goal, or should it become a secondary path after visitors try the quiz?
- Would you prefer the consent card to stay fully explanatory inline, or can its details move behind a disclosure on mobile?
